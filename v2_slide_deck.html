02Act I — Why V2

Every one of 300 replications agreed with every other

V1's headline result was clean — the LLM won overwhelmingly under Medium and Heavy disruption, lost consistently under Light. Auditing why the margin was so unanimous is what produced V2.

What the audit found

In every profile, the mean cost gap between policies was 7.6×–10.2× larger than the random noise between replications — a gap the environment's randomness was never designed to be able to bridge. Win rate was 100%/0% in all three profiles, never once mixed.

  • Shipment release schedule was fully deterministic — exactly 40 units, every day
  • The disruption's timing, duration, and disclosure were fully known in advance
  • Only 5 nodes, 6 edges, ~3 end-to-end routes — not enough structure for a close call

What that means

Not that the LLM's advantage was fake — the paired design and cost model were sound. It means the experiment wasn't random enough to ever produce anything but a foregone conclusion. A result that can't vary isn't evidence of robustness — it's evidence the test never had the power to disagree with itself.

win rate 100%/0% in every profile → the mean effect and the noise floor were never close enough to actually compete
03Act I — Why V2

Three fixed points, three targeted fixes

Nothing about the simulation physics, cost model, action space, or paired-branch fairness mechanism changes. V2 extends the inputs those already-validated mechanisms handle — not the mechanisms themselves.

Fix 1 — Topology

One fixed 5-node network becomes three tiers — Compact / Standard / Extended — crossed independently with severity, so route redundancy itself becomes an experimental variable.

Fix 2 — Randomness

Disruption start day, duration, and information delay are now sampled per replication, not fixed in the scenario file. Shipment quantity is sampled too, on Compact/Extended.

Fix 3 — Taxonomy

Demand spikes/drops and supplier-capacity shocks join the six network-side shock types V1 already had, plus correlated compound events sharing one root cause.

06Act II — What Changed

The fairness mechanism this whole system exists to protect

Every new source of randomness plugs into the same machine V1 already validated. Nothing about how the two policies are compared changed.
  • Four-action space — WAIT / REROUTE / EXPEDITE / ABSTAIN — unchanged
  • The shared validator and fallback chain — unchanged
  • The LLM's five approved read-only tools — unchanged
  • Every cost formula — transport, reroute, expedite, holding, backlog, late, terminal — unchanged
  • One shared random world per replication, drawn entirely before any decision
  • Four cloned branches per replication — heuristic/LLM × undisrupted/disrupted
  • The undisrupted tape still differs from the disrupted one only by the shock(s)
  • TCD = J(disrupted) − J(undisrupted), delta = TCD(LLM) − TCD(heuristic) — unchanged

09Act IV — Results

Topology doesn't just change the margin — it flips the winner

Compact and Standard both trace the pattern V1 first found: heuristic wins Light, LLM dominates by Heavy. Extended draws the opposite line entirely — the heuristic's advantage grows with severity. More structural route redundancy did not make the LLM's job easier; it seems to have made the heuristic's simpler, faster, lowest-estimated-cost search more competitive precisely when there's more to search.

A plausible mechanism, not yet a proven one

The heuristic evaluates every candidate route by lowest estimated cost and never has to reason about which of many plausible paths is worth exploring — with 7 real alternatives, that exhaustive-but-cheap search may simply out-compete a bounded, tool-call-limited agent making judgment calls under more genuine ambiguity. It's also the tier whose severity scenarios target hub_1 rather than port_primary, so the disruption mechanism itself isn't identical to Compact/Standard's — a confound this deck doesn't fully resolve (see Limitations).

15Act V — Close

What this does and doesn't show

What it shows

  • The audit's root cause is genuinely fixed — signal-to-noise dropped from ~9× to under 3× everywhere
  • At least one cell (Compact × Medium) now produces a real, mixed win rate
  • Topology materially changes which policy wins, not just by how much
  • Standard reproduces V1's original pattern, validating the machinery didn't break

What it doesn't show

  • Extended's severity scenarios target hub_1, not port_primary — the reversal is entangled with a different shock target, not topology alone
  • Standard's shipment quantity stays fixed (a deliberate V1 anchor) while Compact/Extended's varies — a real, acknowledged confound on any Standard-vs-other comparison
  • 33 replications/cell (vs. Standard's 100) is real statistical power, but less than V1's original single-profile runs had
  • One model, one temperature, one prompt — this is a policy-design finding, not a claim about LLMs generally
16Act V — Close

Open questions this grid raises, not answers

The most interesting finding here isn't "the LLM wins" or "the LLM loses" — it's that which one wins depends on the shape of the network, in a direction that isn't obvious in advance. That's a real result, and also an invitation.

1Is Extended's reversal about route count, or about the hub_1 vs. port_primary target — a same-target Extended scenario would separate the two
2Does giving Standard the same quantity variance Compact/Extended have change its numbers materially, closing the one acknowledged confound
3Do the demand-side and supplier-side shock types (built, validated, not yet run at grid scale) change the picture further
4Does a larger per-cell replication count on Compact/Extended sharpen or soften Compact × Medium's 75.8/24.2 split
